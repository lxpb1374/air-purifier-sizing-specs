## Start with the room, not the brand

Air purifiers are sold on coverage numbers, and those numbers are optimistic by design. A unit rated for 825 square feet is usually rated at one air change per hour, which is a low bar — air passes through the filter once an hour. For allergy or smoke situations, five air changes per hour is closer to what people actually want.

The same machine in Medify's own published numbers covers 825 sq ft at 1 ACH and 172 sq ft at 4.8 ACH. That's the same box, a difference of roughly 5x, purely because of the assumption behind the number.

You can do the arithmetic yourself. For 5 air changes per hour in a room with 8-foot ceilings:

**CFM needed ≈ room square feet × 0.67**

A 300 sq ft living room needs about 200 CFM of clean air delivery to hit that target. If you'd rather not run the formula, buy a purifier rated for two to three times your actual floor area — that absorbs the gap between the marketing rating and real-world performance with furniture, doors, and people in the way.

The spec that makes this comparable across brands is CADR (Clean Air Delivery Rate), usually in CFM in the US. It's the only apples-to-apples airflow figure in the category, and it's on the box. Coverage claims are not.

## The specs that actually move the price

Four things separate a $170 purifier from a $1,200 one.

**Filtration standard.** True HEPA is tested to capture at least 99.97% of particles down to 0.3 microns — dust, pollen, mold spores, bacteria. If a product page says "HEPA-type," "HEPA-style," or "HEPA-like," it isn't claiming to meet that bar. A Wirecutter summary of a NASA study found HEPA media captures nearly 100% of particles as small as 0.01 microns, which is the reassuring part of the story rather than the marketing part.

**The carbon layer.** HEPA catches particles and nothing else — it doesn't touch gases. Activated carbon handles odors, VOCs, and some combustion gases. The EPA notes that VOC concentrations indoors are typically far higher than outdoors, and that PM2.5 is small enough to work its way into the lungs and bloodstream. A thin carbon sheet on a budget unit is fine for a bathroom that smells like a locker room; it's not going to do much for a kitchen with a gas stove and a cat.

**Formaldehyde handling.** Plywood, particleboard, new furniture, rugs, varnishes, and paint all off-gas formaldehyde. Dyson's own framing is that formaldehyde molecules are 500 times smaller than 0.1 microns, which is why ordinary carbon struggles with them. Machines that genuinely break formaldehyde down use a catalytic filter — Dyson's SCO layer converts it to carbon dioxide and water rather than just trapping it. If you've just moved into a renovation, this is the spec to pay for. If not, it's a few hundred dollars of capability you won't notice.

**Noise.** Manufacturer specs typically quote something like 22–52 dB for a mid-range unit, and the low end is the one you care about if the purifier lives in a bedroom. A machine that only runs quietly because it also barely runs isn't a solution; look for the low-speed figure plus an auto mode and a display that can be dimmed or switched off.

**Is chasing cashback worth it on a $170 purifier?**
Marginally. It's worth doing on a multi-unit order or anything over a few hundred dollars, and not worth delaying a purchase for. The sale price matters more.

**Is "HEPA-type" filtration acceptable?**
It doesn't carry the 99.97% at 0.3 microns certification that True HEPA does. On a tight budget, a genuine True HEPA unit with a smaller coverage rating beats a bigger machine with vague filtration claims.

**Should I buy one big purifier or several small ones?**
Several small ones, in almost every case where doors are involved. Clean air doesn't travel through walls, and three units at roughly $190 will outperform a single unit of the same total price in a multi-room home.

**Do I need a formaldehyde filter?**
Only if your home is new, recently renovated, or full of new furniture and rugs. Otherwise that money buys better coverage or quieter operation instead.
